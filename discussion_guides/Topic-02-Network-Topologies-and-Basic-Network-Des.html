Two Views of Topology: Physical and Logical

concept
📖 What to Read & Display on Screen

Network topology describes how devices and connections are arranged. Physical topology shows the actual layout, while logical topology describes how communication travels.

  • Physical topology: which devices have direct connections and where the cables or wireless links are arranged.
  • Logical topology: how communication uses those connections, including the path and access rules followed by the data.

Six network topologies

concept
📖 What to Read & Display on Screen

Star, bus, ring, mesh, tree, and hybrid networks use different connection patterns. Those patterns affect expansion, cost, available paths, and the impact of failures.

  • Star: endpoints connect to a central switch. A single endpoint cable failure usually isolates that endpoint.
  • Bus: a classic shared coaxial backbone requires correct termination. A backbone break disrupts the segment.
  • Ring: a classic single ring passes traffic along a closed path. A break disrupts this simple model; protected rings may recover.
  • Full mesh: every node connects directly to every other node; alternate paths can survive one link failure.
  • Tree: branches connect hierarchically. An upstream link failure isolates the subtree below it.
  • Hybrid: combines layouts, such as two stars joined by a backbone. The effect of a failure depends on where it occurs.

Bus Topology

example
📖 What to Read & Display on Screen

A bus connects devices to a shared backbone rather than to a central switch. Signals use the common cable, and correct termination is needed at its ends.

  • Each computer attaches through a tap to the same backbone; computers do not forward traffic for their neighbors.
  • This classic-bus model treats a broken backbone as disrupting the entire segment.

Tree Topology

example
📖 What to Read & Display on Screen

A tree arranges connections in levels, with branches below a central root. An upstream link can affect every endpoint in the branch beneath it.

  • Computer A reaches Computer B through Branch A, the core, and Branch B.
  • Removing the core-to-Branch B link isolates Computer B while Branch A keeps its core connection.

Hybrid Topology

example
📖 What to Read & Display on Screen

A hybrid combines connection patterns within one network. This example joins two switched groups through one inter-switch link.

  • Computers in different groups use both switches and the connecting backbone.
  • If that backbone fails, the groups separate; devices on the same working switch can remain connected.

Choosing a Network Topology

concept
📖 What to Read & Display on Screen

A useful topology meets the network’s capacity, coverage, growth, and availability needs. A layout that is easy to expand may still depend on a central device or upstream connection.

  • Count the computers, printers, servers, and uplinks that need connections; allow spare switch ports for growth.
  • Choose suitable cable lengths and record which device uses each port. Clear labels make expansion and fault tracing easier.
  • Consider the effect of one failed link or central device. Extra Ethernet links require appropriate loop prevention rather than simply connecting every available port.
